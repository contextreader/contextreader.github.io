Those who walked every day recovered a little faster, but the difference was not significant (p = 0.21).

Now it’s in your language. It still doesn’t say what “significant” means here.

sig·nif·i·cantadjective
  1. important; worth attention
  2. large enough to be noticed
  3. statisticstoo large to be explained by chance
  4. suggesting a hidden meaning

Four meanings. Which one is this?

1

I read almost everything in English. It isn’t my first language.

I rarely get lost in a whole paragraph. I get lost on one word, often one I already know, used in a way I don’t.

2

Google Translate gives you the words, not the meaning.

It turns the sentence into your language, but it never tells you that “significant” is a statistics term here. You get a translation, not an explanation, and you had to leave the page to get it.

3

A dictionary gives every meaning and leaves the choosing to you.

To pick the right one, you already need to understand the sentence, which is the thing you were trying to do.

4

Context Reader reads the sentence first.

Until recently, no tool could tell which meaning a sentence uses. A language model can. So Context Reader explains that one meaning, in your language, without you leaving the page.

One job: help you read what you need to read, in a language that isn’t your first.

In the bubble

One answer first. Three ways to go further.

The first answer is short on purpose, so you can get back to reading. When you want more, each button asks about the same word in the same paragraph.

Those who walked every day recovered a little faster, but the difference was not significant (p = 0.21).

More: see the word in use.

A short scenario, a dialogue and a story, set in the same world as the page you’re on, in English and in your language.

General: see the whole word.

A general definition plus two other uses in different contexts, each one also in your language. It’s the one button that ignores your sentence on purpose: the whole word, for when you want it.

Simple: hear it more simply.

The same meaning in the plainest words, with one everyday example. For when the explanation itself is the hard part.

EN shows this one answer in English, and your language stays set. Search pronounces the word or looks it up. Esc closes the bubble.

Why a key, and not an account?

  • Free for goodNobody pays us per lookup, because nothing of ours runs. There’s no plan to upgrade to and no limit of ours.
  • Nothing in betweenThe word and its paragraph go from your browser to Google’s Gemini, with your key. No account, no analytics.
  • Your key stays with youIt’s stored on this device and read only by the extension’s background worker. You can lock it with a passphrase.
  • After that, it’s Google’s, not oursYour words arrive at Gemini with your own key, so Google’s terms cover them, not ours. Google says that on the free tier it uses submitted content to improve its products, and that human reviewers may read it with the data disconnected from your account; on a paid key it says it doesn’t. The model was never handed a list of languages.

It learned them from the text it was trained on. That is why the extension can answer in a language nobody wired in by hand, and why we don’t treat languages as a feature list. We asked Gemini itself:

Which is also why it isn’t equally good in every language.

A language the model saw a lot of comes out steadier than one it saw little of. We would rather you heard that from the model than from us:

Where we can help, we do.

English and Sinhala are the tuned two: they ship worked examples, checked by a speaker, that teach the model to pick the sense a field uses. The other 108 work without them, so specialist terms are less reliable there, and the extension says so rather than implying parity. Adding examples for a language is a small edit: a few word pairs a speaker judges right and wrong, never machine-translated.

Privacy

Everything that leaves your machine.

Not a promise: a list. Two requests go out, and both are in the source you can read.

RequestsContext Reader
POSTgenerativelanguage.googleapis.comEach lookup

The word you selected and the paragraph around it, with your own key.

GETfonts.googleapis.com · fonts.gstatic.comChinese, Japanese and Korean only

The typeface those three scripts need, the first time a bubble opens on a page. Every other language reads from faces bundled inside the extension and fetches nothing. Sent with no referrer, so the font servers aren’t told which page you’re on.

no referrer

Nothing else. No analytics, no telemetry, no account.

That is the extension. This website is a different thing: it counts visits with Cloudflare Web Analytics, which is cookieless and sets no identifier. The extension has no analytics of any kind, and never will.

Two requests. That’s the list.

Your lookup goes straight to Google’s Gemini with your key. For most readers that is the only request there is, because the typefaces ship inside the extension. Chinese, Japanese and Korean are the exception: those fetch one font file when a bubble first opens.

One host permission, and what it doesn’t cover.

The extension asks Chrome for exactly one host. Chrome holds it to that.

"host_permissions": [
  "https://generativelanguage.googleapis.com/*"
]

The font request is made by the page itself, not by the extension, so that permission isn’t what stops it going elsewhere; the code is. Bundling the fonts into the extension would remove the request altogether, and that change is still open.

There’s nothing to log in to.

  • No account, no analytics, no telemetry, no identifiers, no crash reports — in the extension. This website counts visits with Cloudflare Web Analytics, cookieless and without identifiers; that is the site, not the product.
  • Your key is stored on this device only, never synced to a Google account, and read only by the extension’s background worker. A passphrase encrypts it at rest.
  • The whole extension is open source under MIT, so none of this has to be taken on trust.
